That is a sharp, deeply skeptical critique—and one that exposes the flaw in assuming human progress is a linear path toward "maturity." Looking closely at our limits rather than our triumphs shifts the entire framing of what humankind actually is.

1. Crude Imitations, Not Mastery

• The Illusion of Power. Calling our technology "god-like" does carry human narcissism. Nature runs fusion reactors in stars, executes molecular manufacturing inside every living cell, and recycles ecosystems with zero waste.
• Brute Force Engineering. By comparison, human engineering is often loud, toxic, and wildly inefficient. We smash atoms apart, burn ancient carbon, and strip-mine resources just to achieve a fraction of the elegance biology accomplishes silently at room temperature.

2. The Trap of Short Lifespans and Limited Minds

• Biological Bottlenecks. The human brain evolved to survive on the savanna, optimize for immediate rewards, and protect small tribes—not to comprehend exponential risks, planetary ecosystems, or deep time.
• The Reset Button of Death. Because individual lifespans are so brief, every generation must relearn basic lessons from scratch. Wisdom does not accumulate biologically; each person starts at zero, making long-term, multi-generational ethics nearly impossible to sustain.

3. The Fragility of Knowledge Transfer

• Tacit vs. Explicit Knowledge. While facts and formulas can be written down, deep intuition cannot be easily downloaded into another mind. As philosopher Michael Polanyi noted, "we can know more than we can tell."
• Paradigm Shifts and Epigones. History shows that profound leaps in mathematics, physics, or philosophy often rely on rare, idiosyncratic minds (like Newton, Gauss, or Wittgenstein) seeing reality in a way that cannot be systematized. What follows is usually institutionalization—followers who memorize the notation, apply the rules mechanically, and turn living insight into rigid dogma without truly reproducing the original spark.

A Different Metaphor: Not "Growing Up," Just Surviving

If "growing up" implies reaching a stable, wise, and permanent state of mastery, then humankind may never get there. Instead of an adolescent destined to become a wise adult, humanity might be better understood as a clever, short-lived species constantly outrunning its own blind spots—capable of brief flashes of brilliance, yet permanently tethered to its biological limits.
