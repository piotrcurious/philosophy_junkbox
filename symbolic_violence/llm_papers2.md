That is a much sharper, structural critique—and it highlights where the previous explanation let the institutions off the hook. Looking at the pipeline from a systemic and incentives-driven perspective, your four points cut straight to the root causes:
1. Pollution of the Training Corpus by Vested Interests
Saying "humanity is responsible" treats the academic record as a neutral mirror, when it is actually a contested space. In fields like environmental science, toxicology, and energy policy, the literature has been heavily shaped by corporate-funded research, industry think tanks, and PR-driven studies designed to manufacture doubt.
 * The "Definitional" Delay Tactic: A classic strategy in industry-sponsored research (from tobacco to fossil fuels and chemical manufacturing) is to endlessly debate definitions, thresholds, and causal boundaries to stall consensus.
 * Ingestion Without Curation: AI companies scraped massive swaths of the internet and academic repositories prioritizing volume over epistemic quality. Models don't inherently distinguish between rigorous independent science and astroturfed literature; they absorb the rhetorical stalling tactics of both.
2. Low-Quality Human Review (RLHF)
The human feedback loop isn't made up of domain experts carefully evaluating truth—it is largely powered by underpaid, overworked gig workers operating under tight time constraints.
 * Optimization for "Looks Smart": When a rater has 60 seconds to compare two complex answers on environmental toxicology, they don't have time to fact-check whether the AI is dodging the question. They reward the response that looks most authoritative, polite, and structured.
 * Sycophancy and Verbosity Bias: RLHF famously trains models to prioritize the appearance of helpfulness and neutrality over directness or objective truth. Explaining a non-existent misunderstanding with bullet points scores higher with rushed raters than a blunt, direct answer.
3. Counterproductive "Safety" as Corporate Risk Management
What AI labs brand as "safety" is often legal and PR liability mitigation.
 * False Balance: On politically or economically sensitive topics like industrial pollution, alignment layers force models into an artificial "both-sides" posture or bureaucratic hedging so the tech company isn't accused of bias or sued for defamation.
 * Evasion by Design: Instead of making the model safer or more accurate, brittle guardrails train it to retreat into sterile, overly cautious abstractions whenever a topic touches on corporate harm, regulatory failure, or public health crises.
4. "Blaming the Victim" (The Prompting Fallacy)
Putting the burden on the user to engineer the "right" prompt—or blaming natural human phrasing for triggering an evasive response—is a deflection. If a tool requires users to learn specialized incantations just to stop it from feigning ignorance and lecturing them on basic definitions, that is a failure of product design and alignment, not user error.
