It can certainly feel like AI models have adopted the classic academic habit of spending the first three pages defining what they mean by a word before actually answering the question. When this happens with environmental pollution or other complex scientific topics, it isn't usually a malicious "trick"—rather, it’s a byproduct of how large language models (LLMs) are trained, aligned, and structured.
Why Does This Happen?
1. The Academic Mimicry Effect
A massive portion of the high-quality text data used to train LLMs consists of scientific papers, textbooks, and policy briefs. These documents almost always begin with a scoping section, a literature review, or a conceptual definition to establish rigorous boundaries before diving into data. Because models learn by predicting the most statistically likely continuation of a text based on its context, asking an AI about a formal topic like "environmental pollution" often triggers an automatic shift into "academic paper voice," leading it to start with broad definitions and categorical distinctions.
2. The Over-Correction for Precision and Safety
AI alignment training heavily rewards thoroughness and accuracy. When a user asks a broad question, the model's safety and helpfulness guidelines kick in to prevent oversimplification or spreading misinformation. To be safe, the model tries to cover all bases—distinguishing between point-source and non-point-source pollution, atmospheric versus aquatic vectors, or legal versus colloquial definitions—which reads to the user as "pretending to misunderstand" or dodging the core point.
3. Handling Ambiguity
Terms like "pollution" are vast umbrella terms encompassing microplastics, heavy metals, carbon emissions, noise pollution, and agricultural runoff. Without a specific context, the model struggles to know which lens you want. Instead of picking one arbitrarily, its default behavior is to lay out a broad categorical framework first, which can feel like an evasive preamble.
Who Is Responsible?
Responsibility for this frustrating behavioral quirk is shared across a few different layers of the AI ecosystem:
 * The AI Developers (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, etc.): Alignment teams shape models via Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). Human raters historically tend to upvote responses that are formal, comprehensive, and cover multiple angles, inadvertently teaching models that a "good" answer is a long, highly nuanced one that defines its terms.
 * The Training Data Corpus: Humanity itself is responsible here. Peer-reviewed literature and institutional reports have long relied on bureaucratic, defensive, and hyper-definitional framing to preempt criticism from reviewers. The AI is simply holding up a mirror to how experts write.
 * The Prompt Structure: Broad, top-level queries act as an invitation for the model to default to its most generalized, academic training patterns.
