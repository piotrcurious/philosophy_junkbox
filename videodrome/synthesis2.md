The Gamified Social Order as a Category Error

A Philosophical, Mathematical, and Empirical Critique of Proxy Optimization, Structural Coercion, and the Fiction of the Universal Player

Executive Abstract

Contemporary society increasingly describes human activity in the language of games: people are told to optimize their careers, maximize their productivity, accumulate credentials, improve their rankings, increase their “engagement,” build their personal brands, manage their credit profiles, optimize their portfolios, and continuously improve measurable outputs.

The metaphor appears harmless because games really do contain goals, rules, incentives, scores, strategies, feedback, and winners. But the analogy breaks precisely where it matters most.

A game is a voluntarily entered formal activity in which participants adopt artificial constraints for the sake of pursuing an internally specified objective. Human existence is not ordinarily entered voluntarily, its constitutive institutions are neither fixed nor equally controllable by all participants, exit can carry catastrophic costs, and the physical and social state of the world cannot simply be reset. The crucial difference is therefore not that human beings “fail to play the game correctly.” It is that survival, social reproduction, ecological dependence, and political power are being represented as though they constituted a game in the first place.

This essay develops that criticism through five complementary traditions.

Diogenes of Sinope supplies the epistemological weapon: deface the currency of conventional valuation and ask what remains when socially circulating symbols of worth are stripped away. The ancient Cynic opposition between nomos and physis provides a particularly powerful precedent for interrogating artificial social valuations. The historical tradition surrounding to nomisma paracharattein—“alter” or “deface the currency”—is complicated, but its later philosophical interpretation became a metaphor for challenging conventional values.

Bourdieu explains why such conventions become effective: domination does not require constant physical violence when classifications imposed by institutions are internalized as natural descriptions of the world. What appears to the individual as personal failure can therefore be produced by an institutional field whose rules, resources, and starting positions are highly unequal.

Proudhon and Piketty expose the material structure beneath the scoreboard. Proudhon's concept of force collective identifies productive capacities that emerge from coordinated social labor but cannot be reduced to the sum of isolated individual contributions. Piketty's r-g mechanism identifies one important condition under which wealth inequality can persist or intensify, while also emphasizing that r>g is not a deterministic law guaranteeing infinite inequality.

Russell, Goodhart, and Campbell supply the logic of proxy failure. Institutions frequently optimize measurable indicators rather than the realities those indicators imperfectly represent. A scalar target can therefore become an independent behavioral attractor. Goodhart's original observation concerned the instability of statistical relationships under policy control; its familiar generalized form captures what happens when an indicator becomes a target. Campbell similarly warned that heavily consequential quantitative indicators become vulnerable to corruption and distortion.

Euler, Whitney, Arrow, and Braess provide formal models of a different but related phenomenon: local rationality does not imply global optimality. Greedy optimization is globally reliable only under particular structural conditions such as matroidal independence. Arrow demonstrates the limitations of preference aggregation under specific axioms. Braess demonstrates that independently optimized routing can produce an equilibrium inferior to another feasible configuration. None of these theorems proves that society itself is mathematically inconsistent. They instead provide precise models for the failure of the intuition that a collection of individually rational optimizers necessarily produces a rational whole.

Finally, Ole Peters, comprehensive wealth accounting, and contemporary inequality research shift attention from short-term flows and ensemble averages toward trajectories, stocks, constraints, and distribution. Peters' ergodicity critique shows why ensemble averages can differ systematically from the time-average experience of an individual under multiplicative dynamics. The World Bank's Changing Wealth of Nations 2024 explicitly complements GDP with produced, human, natural, and financial wealth and warns that GDP alone cannot establish whether an economy is accumulating or depleting its productive base. The World Inequality Report 2026 reports that the global top 0.001%—roughly 56,000 adults—held about three times as much personal wealth as the bottom half of humanity in 2025.

The argument therefore does not require the claim that every market, every metric, or every institution is intrinsically invalid. The stronger and more defensible proposition is narrower:

«A society that systematically converts survival, social membership, ecological dependence, and human development into competitive proxy-optimization problems commits a category error.»

The fundamental problem is not that the players are insufficiently rational.

It is that the world is not a scoreboard, survival is not voluntary play, and optimization of a proxy is not equivalent to optimization of the reality being proxied.

I. Diogenes and Bourdieu: Defacing the Scoreboard

1. Diogenes and the Invention of Counter-Valuation

The Cynic tradition begins with an attack on the authority of conventional value.

The phrase associated with Diogenes, to nomisma paracharattein, is conventionally rendered as “deface the currency” or “alter the currency.” The historical story is uncertain: ancient sources disagree about the precise nature of the episode, and modern scholarship disputes how literally the monetary episode should be understood. But the metaphorical interpretation became central to the Cynic tradition: conventional values themselves could be treated as counterfeit currency.

This is much deeper than simply saying that money is bad.

The Cynic question is:

[
\text{Who authorized this value?}
]

And immediately after it:

[
\text{What happens when I refuse to recognize it?}
]

Modern societies contain enormous systems of conventional valuation:

[
M=
{
\text{income},
\text{net worth},
\text{credit score},
\text{credentials},
\text{productivity},
\text{followers},
\text{engagement},
\text{ratings},
\text{rankings},
\text{market price}
}.
]

None of these quantities is necessarily meaningless.

The problem begins when the metric ceases to be an instrument for describing something and becomes an authority for determining what the thing is.

A credit score can predict repayment behavior.

It cannot determine whether a human being is trustworthy in every morally relevant sense.

A salary can measure an exchange relationship.

It cannot measure the total value of a person's existence.

A follower count can measure audience size.

It cannot measure whether the resulting social relation is meaningful.

A GDP statistic can measure market production.

It cannot, by itself, establish whether the productive base of a society is being accumulated or depleted. The World Bank explicitly makes this distinction in its comprehensive-wealth framework.

The Cynic intervention therefore consists in a reversal:

[
M \not\equiv Q
]

where M is a measure and Q is the underlying quality.

The institutional error occurs when:

[
\max M
]

quietly replaces:

[
\max Q.
]

At that moment the instrument becomes the objective.

The scoreboard has eaten the game.

And then, eventually, the scoreboard eats the player.

2. The Lantern and the Search for the Human Being

The famous image of Diogenes carrying a lantern in daylight in search of “a human being” belongs to a later and highly mediated tradition, but it expresses something philosophically useful: social recognition and human reality are not identical. Ancient sources themselves present Diogenes as deliberately confronting convention and opposing conventional status to a life according to nature.

Applied to contemporary institutions, the lantern becomes an epistemological instrument.

The question is no longer:

«What is your rank?»

but:

«What remains of the person when the ranking mechanism is removed?»

Not:

«What is your productivity score?»

but:

«What happens to the person's life when productivity is no longer the unit of legitimacy?»

Not:

«What is your market value?»

but:

«What capacities, relationships, knowledge, memories, skills, and forms of life exist independently of their exchange price?»

This is the beginning of de-gamification.

It is not rejection of measurement.

It is rejection of the ontological promotion of measurement.

II. Bourdieu: When the Scoreboard Becomes Internal

The power of a metric increases when people cease to experience it as an external command.

The most effective classification system eventually appears natural.

A person does not need to be physically forced to regard themselves as a failure if the institutional environment has already taught them to interpret unemployment, low income, lack of credentials, poor credit, low social-media engagement, or absence of prestige as evidence of personal inadequacy.

Here the Cynic critique intersects with Bourdieu's analysis of symbolic domination.

The crucial transformation is:

[
\text{institutional arrangement}
\rightarrow
\text{social classification}
\rightarrow
\text{internalized classification}
\rightarrow
\text{self-judgment}.
]

Structural conditions are converted into psychological narratives.

A housing shortage becomes:

«“I failed to buy.”»

A labor-market transformation becomes:

«“I failed to remain competitive.”»

An impossible productivity regime becomes:

«“I have poor time management.”»

An algorithmic ranking system becomes:

«“Other people are simply better than me.”»

The institutional structure disappears from consciousness.

Only the player remains.

That is the ideological achievement of gamification: the transformation of structural relations into apparently individual optimization problems.

III. Life Is Not a Game

1. The Suits Problem

The strongest philosophical version of the argument should not claim that Bernard Suits defines games by four properties including “resetability.” He does not.

Suits' analysis instead revolves around a much more interesting structure: games involve the voluntary adoption of obstacles whose artificial difficulty is constitutive of the activity. The player does not merely desire the outcome; the player accepts a particular inefficient route to that outcome because the imposed constraints are part of what makes the activity a game. Contemporary interpretations of Suits retain this emphasis on voluntary acceptance of unnecessary obstacles.

This yields an important distinction.

Suppose a marathon runner voluntarily refuses to take a car to the finish line.

That inefficiency is not an unfortunate defect of the marathon.

It is the point.

The runner is choosing:

[
\text{goal}+\text{constitutive constraint}
]

rather than merely:

[
\min(\text{time to destination}).
]

The game is therefore partly defined by the player's consent to its artificial constraints.

This is almost the exact opposite of compulsory socioeconomic optimization.

---

2. The Four Missing Conditions

The most useful contrast is not to invent a definition of “game,” but to identify properties of many ordinary games that become badly distorted when transported to social survival.

Voluntary adoption

The player normally chooses to participate.

The worker does not normally choose whether to require food.

The child does not choose whether to require shelter.

The individual does not choose whether their society has housing markets, taxation systems, legal institutions, labor markets, or monetary arrangements.

Thus:

[
\text{choice to play}\neq\text{choice to exist}.
]

Artificial constraints

In a game, constraints are constitutive.

In ordinary life, many constraints are imposed by physical reality or by institutions one did not individually design.

A chess player voluntarily refuses to move a bishop orthogonally.

A person cannot voluntarily refuse the laws of thermodynamics.

A game rule says:

[
\text{bishop} \not\rightarrow \text{orthogonal movement}.
]

Biophysical reality says:

[
\text{organism} \rightarrow \text{metabolism}.
]

Confusing these two types of constraint is a category error.

Exit

The ability to quit is not absolute in every game, but it is normally meaningful in a way that does not involve forfeiting the basic material conditions of existence.

One can stop playing chess.

One cannot “quit” metabolism.

One can leave an employer.

One cannot simply opt out of food, shelter, health, social dependence, or ecological systems.

Thus the phrase “just play differently” frequently conceals the difference between strategic exit and material nonexistence.

Resetability

Resetability is not a defining philosophical criterion of games, but it is a crucial property of engineered games and simulations.

A chess position can be reconstructed.

A game can be restarted.

A failed strategy can be discarded.

Human decisions often cannot.

A depleted aquifer does not reload.

A destroyed forest does not return when someone presses:

[
\texttt{RESET}.
]

A decade of lost education cannot simply be restored to the state before the policy decision.

A dead ecosystem has no menu option labelled “restart from checkpoint.”

This gives the central asymmetry:

[
\text{game state} \rightarrow \text{often reversible}
]

whereas

[
\text{social/ecological state} \rightarrow \text{often path-dependent and partially irreversible}.
]

Human existence is therefore not merely a game with unusually high stakes.

It is a different ontological category.

IV. Proudhon: Who Owns the Board?

Proudhon's most useful contribution is not the slogan “property is theft” treated as a universal metaphysical proposition.

It is his analysis of collective productive power.

In What Is Property?, Proudhon argues that the productive output of coordinated workers can exceed the sum of individually measured contributions. He explicitly describes a “collective force” that remains after the individual laborers have been paid.

This matters enormously for the game metaphor.

In a conventional game, the board is usually external to the players.

Nobody asks whether one player privately owns the chessboard and charges the others a fee for occupying squares.

In an economy, however, control over the underlying infrastructure can itself be concentrated:

[
\text{land}
+
\text{housing}
+
\text{infrastructure}
+
\text{intellectual property}
+
\text{platforms}
+
\text{financial claims}.
]

The important distinction is between:

[
\text{productive return}
]

and

[
\text{income arising from control over a scarce or exclusionary asset}.
]

These categories overlap in complicated ways; not every return on an asset is pure economic rent. But scarcity rents, monopoly rents, land rents, and various forms of control income cannot simply be collapsed into wages for productive labor.

The “game” metaphor conceals this because it imagines everyone as an equivalent player.

But participants may occupy radically different positions:

[
\text{player}
\qquad
\text{versus}
\qquad
\text{owner of the board}
\qquad
\text{versus}
\qquad
\text{designer of the rules}.
]

These are not equivalent roles.

V. Piketty and the Limits of r>g

Piketty's r>g mechanism should be used carefully.

The crude version says:

[
r>g
\Rightarrow
\text{inequality inevitably explodes}.
]

That is too strong.

Piketty himself explicitly rejected the interpretation that r>g alone mechanically guarantees indefinite increases in wealth inequality. He describes the r-g differential as an important force amplifying wealth inequality, while emphasizing institutions, taxation, shocks, inherited structures, and the broader dynamics of wealth accumulation.

The stronger formulation is:

[
r-g>0
]

can create a differential accumulation channel.

If existing wealth generates returns faster than the economy grows, wealth holders can increase their claims on future income unless countervailing mechanisms—taxation, dilution, consumption, redistribution, inflation, capital losses, political intervention, or other institutional processes—interrupt the process.

The important point for the present argument is not that r>g is a magical inequality theorem.

It is that the economic “game” possesses feedback loops between initial position and future optimization capacity.

A person with capital can use capital to purchase:

- more assets,
- better financial information,
- legal expertise,
- favorable financing,
- geographic mobility,
- risk diversification,
- additional productive capacity.

The person without capital must often use labor income merely to reproduce present existence.

Thus:

[
\text{wealth}
\rightarrow
\text{optionality}
\rightarrow
\text{risk capacity}
\rightarrow
\text{additional wealth}.
]

The reverse process can also occur:

[
\text{income shock}
\rightarrow
\text{debt}
\rightarrow
\text{forced sale}
\rightarrow
\text{loss of assets}
\rightarrow
\text{lower future optionality}.
]

That is not a level playing field.

It is a dynamical system with state-dependent transition probabilities.

VI. The Worker Inside the Control Loop

The gamification metaphor becomes particularly misleading when contemporary management systems actually begin implementing game-like feedback mechanisms.

Here the metaphor becomes literal technology.

The European Commission's 2024–2025 AIMWORK survey covered 70,316 workers across all EU Member States. It found that 37% of employees reported employer use of AI or other tools to monitor working hours, while 24% reported that their schedules were set automatically. The Commission's Joint Research Centre describes digital monitoring and algorithmic management as rapidly evolving fields and reports significant exposure across European workplaces.

This creates a feedback architecture:

[
x_t
\rightarrow
\text{measurement}
\rightarrow
\text{score}
\rightarrow
\text{algorithmic decision}
\rightarrow
\text{behavior}
\rightarrow
x_{t+1}.
]

The worker is not merely playing.

The worker is being measured by an adaptive controller.

The controller observes behavior x_t, computes a metric m_t, modifies incentives u_t, and thereby changes the next state:

[
x_{t+1}=F(x_t,u_t,\epsilon_t).
]

The institution is therefore not simply a referee.

It is part of the dynamical system.

That distinction matters.

VII. Russell: The Logical Error of Scalarization

Suppose human life is described by a state vector:

[
x\in\mathbb{R}^n.
]

The vector might include:

[
x=
(
income,
health,
time,
relationships,
skills,
autonomy,
ecological security,
housing,
community,
knowledge,
physical environment,
future options,
\ldots
).
]

A scalar metric is then some function

[
m=f(x).
]

Scalarization is not intrinsically wrong.

Engineering routinely requires scalar objectives.

The problem occurs when:

[
m=f(x)
]

is treated as though m were a sufficient representation of everything relevant about x.

For n\gg1, many distinct states can have the same scalar value:

[
x_1\neq x_2
]

while

[
f(x_1)=f(x_2).
]

Therefore:

[
f(x)
]

cannot generally preserve all information relevant to the original state.

This is not a moral argument.

It is elementary information loss.

The crucial institutional question is therefore:

«Is the scalar a sufficient statistic for the decision being made?»

If not, maximizing it creates an optimization problem whose objective function has silently discarded dimensions of the underlying reality.

That is the deeper meaning of metric inflation.

VIII. Goodhart, Campbell, and Proxy Capture

Goodhart's original observation concerned the instability of a statistical regularity once it becomes an object of control. The popularized formulation is:

[
\text{When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure}.
]

Campbell expressed a related problem for quantitative social indicators: once indicators are used heavily in decision-making, they become vulnerable to corruption and distortion.

The common mechanism is:

[
M\approx Q
]

becomes

[
\max M
]

and then:

[
M\not\approx Q.
]

The system has created an incentive for agents to optimize the representation rather than the represented object.

Examples are familiar:

[
\text{test score}\neq\text{learning}
]

[
\text{billable hours}\neq\text{social usefulness}
]

[
\text{engagement}\neq\text{meaningful communication}
]

[
\text{hospital throughput}\neq\text{health}
]

[
\text{GDP growth}\neq\text{sustainable wealth accumulation}
]

[
\text{employee activity}\neq\text{productive human contribution}.
]

The crucial insight is that gaming need not involve dishonesty.

An intelligent agent will optimize whatever objective actually determines its consequences.

Thus the problem can emerge even when every participant behaves perfectly rationally.

IX. The “Fallacy of Symmetrism”

“Symmetrism” is useful here, but it should be presented explicitly as a name for the analytical error identified in this essay, rather than as a standard formal fallacy attributable to Russell.

The error consists of representing structurally asymmetric relations as if they were symmetric interactions between equivalent players.

Formally:

[
A\leftrightarrow B
]

is represented as though both parties possess comparable:

- exit options,
- bargaining power,
- information,
- ownership,
- time horizons,
- fallback positions,
- capacity to absorb losses.

But in many real situations:

[
P_A\gg P_B
]

where P represents bargaining power.

Consider:

[
\text{platform}
\leftrightarrow
\text{gig worker}.
]

A platform may change the algorithm.

The worker cannot change the platform.

Or:

[
\text{landlord}
\leftrightarrow
\text{tenant}.
]

The landlord may own multiple properties.

The tenant may possess only the necessity of shelter.

Or:

[
\text{industrial polluter}
\leftrightarrow
\text{local population}.
]

The firm may possess capital, lawyers, insurance, and geographic mobility.

Residents cannot relocate an aquifer.

Calling all these relationships “market transactions” is descriptively correct at one level and insufficient at another.

The transaction can be voluntary in the narrow legal sense while occurring under highly asymmetric constraint.

The key distinction is:

[
\text{formal consent}
\neq
\text{symmetrical power}.
]

The worker may be free to choose which employer to work for while lacking the freedom to stop selling labor altogether.

The tenant may be free to choose among landlords while lacking the freedom to cease needing shelter.

The consumer may be free to choose among platforms while lacking the economic ability to exit digital infrastructure entirely.

Thus:

[
\text{choice between suppliers}
\neq
\text{choice whether the underlying necessity exists}.
]

This is the core of the anti-symmetrism argument.

X. Euler and Whitney: The Mathematics of Local Rationality

The most productive use of Euler, Whitney, and combinatorial optimization is not to pretend that society literally is a matroid.

It is to demonstrate a general mathematical principle:

«Local optimization is globally reliable only under particular structural conditions.»

For a matroid

[
\mathcal{M}=(E,\mathcal I),
]

the independence axioms, particularly the augmentation property, impose exactly the structural regularity required for greedy optimization to work.

The Rado–Edmonds theorem gives the relevant result: for the standard weighted independence setting, greedy optimization succeeds for every weight assignment precisely when the underlying independence system has the relevant matroid structure.

That theorem does not imply:

[
\text{society is non-matroidal}
\Rightarrow
\text{social collapse}.
]

That would be an invalid inference.

The correct analogy is:

[
\boxed{
\text{greedy correctness requires structure}
}
]

and therefore:

[
\boxed{
\text{many independent greedy optimizers do not automatically produce global optimality}
}.
]

To model society more rigorously, define a system state:

[
x_t\in X
]

with constraints:

[
C_j(x_t)\ge0
]

and individual objectives:

[
J_i(x_t).
]

The institution may produce:

[
x_{t+1}

F\left(x_t,
a_1^\ast,\ldots,a_n^\ast
\right)
]

where

[
a_i^\ast

\arg\max_{a_i}J_i(a_i;x_t).
]

The social objective, however, might be something else:

[
W(x_t)

W(
\text{health},
\text{autonomy},
\text{distribution},
\text{ecological integrity},
\text{knowledge},
\text{security},
\text{future options}
).
]

There is no mathematical reason why

[
\arg\max_i J_i
]

must coincide with

[
\arg\max W.
]

Indeed, once externalities, congestion, irreversibility, information asymmetry, and state-dependent constraints appear, divergence is entirely expected.

That is the mathematically serious version of the critique.

XI. Braess's Paradox: When More Choice Produces a Worse System

Braess's paradox offers an unusually clean illustration.

In a network where users independently minimize their own travel time, adding a new link can cause the resulting equilibrium to produce higher travel time for everyone under appropriate network and demand conditions. This is a standard example of the difference between individual optimization and system optimization.

The general structure is:

[
\text{individual best response}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{system optimum}.
]

The lesson is not “markets are Braess paradoxes.”

The lesson is more general:

[
\boxed{
\text{more individually available options}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{better collective state}.
}
]

The same mathematical architecture appears whenever:

- agents share congestible resources;
- one person's action changes the cost faced by another;
- agents optimize private rather than total cost;
- feedback alters the environment being optimized.

Once these conditions exist, simply telling everyone to “optimize better” can worsen the aggregate result.

The system requires coordination, pricing of externalities, constraints, information sharing, or another mechanism that changes the game itself.

XII. Arrow: Preference Aggregation Is Not a Trivial Problem

Arrow's theorem must likewise be used precisely.

It does not prove that democracy is impossible.

It does not prove that collective decisions are meaningless.

It does not prove that every political institution is inconsistent.

Arrow establishes that, under a specified domain of individual ordinal preferences and a specified collection of conditions—including unrestricted domain, Pareto/unanimity, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship—there is no social welfare function satisfying all those conditions simultaneously when there are at least three alternatives.

Its relevance here is methodological.

There is no obvious universal function:

[
F:
(P_1,P_2,\ldots,P_n)
\rightarrow
P_{\text{society}}
]

that transparently converts heterogeneous human preferences into one coherent social ordering while satisfying all the intuitive fairness requirements simultaneously.

Social coordination therefore requires institutional choices about:

- what counts as a preference;
- which dimensions are comparable;
- which rights are non-tradeable;
- which constraints are lexically prior;
- how conflicts are resolved;
- who possesses veto power;
- how future generations are represented;
- how ecological systems enter the decision procedure.

The “invisible optimizer” does not exist.

Someone has to specify the objective function.

XIII. The Social Contract and Contradictory Institutional Objectives

The claim that the contemporary social contract is literally an inconsistent formal system is too strong without constructing a formal theory whose axioms entail:

[
P\land\neg P.
]

But there is a more powerful empirical claim available:

«Contemporary institutions often combine objectives whose simultaneous realization is structurally difficult, and then conceal those conflicts by assigning individuals responsibility for resolving them through personal optimization.»

Consider housing.

One institutional logic treats housing as:

[
\text{asset}
\rightarrow
\text{collateral}
\rightarrow
\text{investment}
\rightarrow
\text{appreciating wealth}.
]

Another treats housing as:

[
\text{basic necessity}
\rightarrow
\text{access requirement}
\rightarrow
\text{social reproduction}.
]

These objectives need not constitute a formal contradiction.

But they can generate a persistent distributional conflict:

[
\text{higher asset valuation}
]

can be good for existing owners while simultaneously increasing the entry cost faced by non-owners.

Likewise:

[
\text{scarcity}
\rightarrow
\text{high asset value}
]

can benefit the owner of a scarce asset while harming the person who requires access to it.

The same physical object can therefore occupy two incompatible positions in two different objective functions.

The institutional system does not necessarily fail because logic itself has been violated.

It can fail because different actors are optimizing different objective functions over the same physical substrate.

That distinction is crucial.

XIV. Compound Claims and Finite Reality

A similar problem occurs between financial claims and physical reality.

Suppose a claim on future output grows according to:

[
C_{t+1}=(1+r)C_t
]

while the real productive base grows according to:

[
Y_{t+1}=(1+g)Y_t.
]

If:

[
r>g
]

over a sufficiently long period, then claims grow faster than the aggregate quantity against which those claims ultimately have to be settled.

That does not mean that physical production must immediately grow at r.

The system has other mechanisms:

- defaults,
- debt restructuring,
- taxation,
- inflation,
- capital losses,
- redistribution,
- changes in ownership,
- productivity gains,
- new investment,
- currency depreciation.

But the mathematical tension remains:

[
\frac{C_t}{Y_t}

\frac{C_0}{Y_0}
\left(
\frac{1+r}{1+g}
\right)^t.
]

When r>g,

[
\frac{C_t}{Y_t}\rightarrow\infty
]

in the simple perpetual-compounding model.

Therefore some variable must eventually change.

The mistake is to imagine that nothing has changed merely because the spreadsheet continues to display compound growth.

A financial claim is not itself food.

A bond is not groundwater.

An equity share is not soil.

A derivative is not a forest.

Financial wealth represents claims upon an underlying network of institutions and productive capacities.

The distinction between the claim and the substrate is fundamental.

XV. World Bank: Wealth Is a Stock, GDP Is a Flow

This is where the World Bank's Changing Wealth of Nations 2024 becomes particularly important.

GDP measures current production and income flows.

Comprehensive wealth instead attempts to measure the stock of assets that support future production and welfare, including:

[
W

K_{\text{produced}}
+
K_{\text{human}}
+
K_{\text{natural}}
+
NFA,
]

with methodological qualifications concerning valuation and asset coverage. The 2024 edition explicitly emphasizes produced capital, human capital, renewable and nonrenewable natural capital, and net foreign assets. It also stresses that GDP cannot establish whether growth is being achieved through accumulation or depletion of the underlying productive base.

This changes the accounting question.

Instead of asking merely:

[
\Delta GDP>0?
]

we can ask:

[
\Delta W_{\text{real, per capita}}>0?
]

The difference is profound.

An economy may produce more this year while degrading:

- groundwater,
- forests,
- soil,
- biodiversity,
- physical infrastructure,
- human capabilities,
- public health.

The World Bank therefore identifies non-declining real wealth per capita as a sustainability criterion, while emphasizing that natural assets can become scarce and have limited substitutes.

The important philosophical consequence is:

«A flow can increase while the stock generating future flows decreases.»

That is the macroeconomic version of spending one's capital while calling the expenditure “income.”

XVI. The Ecosystem Is Not a Spreadsheet

This problem becomes even sharper ecologically.

Suppose a forest is represented only by its timber value:

[
F\rightarrow P_{\text{timber}}.
]

The scalar representation can omit:

[
(
\text{water retention},
\text{soil stability},
\text{carbon storage},
\text{microclimate},
\text{habitat},
\text{nutrient cycling},
\text{recreation},
\text{genetic diversity}
).
]

The problem is therefore not simply “money is bad.”

It is:

[
\pi:
\mathbb{R}^{n}
\rightarrow
\mathbb{R}
]

is many-to-one.

A decision based only on \pi(x) can treat two states as equivalent even when their future transition dynamics are dramatically different.

That becomes especially dangerous where ecological systems possess thresholds.

Let:

[
K_t
]

be an ecological stock.

A simplified transition can be written:

[
K_{t+1}

K_t
+
R(K_t)

E_t,
]

where R(K) is regeneration and E_t extraction.

If regeneration falls sharply below a threshold K^\ast,

[
R'(K)
]

or even the qualitative form of the dynamics may change.

The system can therefore exhibit:

[
K_t>K^\ast
\Rightarrow
\text{stable regeneration}
]

but

[
K_t<K^\ast
\Rightarrow
\text{degradation}.
]

A one-year profit maximizer may not “see” this phase transition.

A physical ecosystem does not care whether the quarterly KPI was green.

---

XVII. Ole Peters and the Problem of the Ensemble Player

The ergodicity problem exposes another deep flaw in the gamified interpretation of economic life.

For an additive process, ensemble averages can sometimes provide a good approximation to the long-run trajectory of an individual.

For multiplicative processes, this can fail dramatically.

Consider geometric Brownian motion:

[
dx=x(\mu,dt+\sigma,dW_t).
]

Its solution is:

[
x(t)

x(0)
\exp
\left[
\left(
\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}
\right)t
+
\sigma W_t
\right].
]

The expected ensemble growth rate is related to \mu, whereas the long-run time-average logarithmic growth rate is:

[
g_{\text{time}}

\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}.
]

Thus:

[
g_{\text{time}}
<
g_{\text{ensemble}}
]

when \sigma>0.

Peters' central point is not that every economic process has exactly this form. It is that the dynamical structure has to be specified before an expectation value can legitimately be interpreted as the growth experience of an individual.

This destroys a common rhetorical trick of aggregate economics:

«“The economy grew, therefore the individual should expect to become richer.”»

That inference does not follow automatically.

An ensemble can become richer while individual trajectories remain highly unequal.

The difference between:

[
E[x_t]
]

and

[
\exp(E[\ln x_t])
]

is not a philosophical curiosity.

It is a statement about how averages behave under multiplicative dynamics.

---

XVIII. Inequality Is Not a Failure of Individual Strategy

The empirical scale of contemporary wealth concentration reinforces the distinction.

The World Inequality Report 2026 estimates that in 2025:

[
\text{top 10%}
\approx
75%
\text{ of global wealth}
]

while:

[
\text{bottom 50%}
\approx
2%.
]

At the extreme end, approximately 56,000 adults in the global top 0.001% held about three times as much wealth as the entire bottom half of humanity combined.

These numbers do not by themselves prove that the distribution is unjust.

They do establish that the phrase “everyone is simply playing the same game” is an extraordinarily poor descriptive model of the actual distribution of resources.

The initial conditions are not remotely equivalent.

Nor are the transition functions.

Nor are the available strategies.

Nor are the costs of failure.

A player who begins with:

[
W_0=10^9
]

does not inhabit the same feasible strategy space as someone beginning with:

[
W_0=10^3.
]

The second player's optimization problem is dominated by survival constraints that may be almost irrelevant to the first.

The state variable matters.

The player's position changes the game.

And when players can influence the rules, the metaphor of a neutral game becomes even less accurate.

---

XIX. The Wealth-Transfer Machine

The same distinction appears in inheritance.

Cerulli Associates projects approximately $124 trillion in wealth transfers through 2048 in the U.S. high-net-worth and ultra-high-net-worth market, with more than half of the projected transfer volume originating from households representing roughly 2% of households.

This does not demonstrate that every inherited fortune is socially illegitimate.

It demonstrates something more basic:

[
\text{starting conditions are transmitted}.
]

A person's optimization problem is not generated from zero at birth.

It inherits:

[
W_0,
\quad
H_0,
\quad
N_0,
\quad
E_0,
\quad
C_0,
\quad
R_0,
]

where wealth, human capital, networks, geography, education, and resilience can all have inherited or family-mediated components.

Therefore the popular narrative:

[
\text{outcome}

f(\text{individual strategy})
]

is incomplete.

A more realistic formulation is:

[
x_{t+1}

F(x_t,a_t,\mathcal I_t,\epsilon_t)
]

where \mathcal I_t represents institutions and inherited state conditions.

The individual's strategy is one variable among several.

It is not the entire system.

---

XX. Surveillance Capitalism and the Conversion of Life Into Data

The gamification system reaches its technological extreme when behavior itself becomes a raw material for optimization.

Digital platforms transform actions into measurable traces:

[
\text{attention}
\rightarrow
\text{data}
\rightarrow
\text{prediction}
\rightarrow
\text{intervention}
\rightarrow
\text{new behavior}
\rightarrow
\text{more data}.
]

The loop becomes:

[
x_t
\rightarrow
d_t
\rightarrow
\hat{x}{t+1}
\rightarrow
u_t
\rightarrow
x{t+1}.
]

This is no longer merely a scoreboard.

It is a feedback controller.

The difference between a traditional bureaucratic metric and an adaptive digital system is therefore profound.

A paper form records behavior.

An adaptive platform can change the incentives that produce the next behavior.

The metric becomes causal.

The measurement infrastructure enters the dynamics of the phenomenon being measured.

That is the most technologically advanced version of Goodhart's problem.

The system does not merely observe the player.

It modifies the player.

---

XXI. From Game Theory to Control Theory

The deepest conceptual correction may therefore be this:

«Contemporary institutions are often better modeled as multi-agent control systems than as games.»

Let:

[
x_t
]

denote social state.

Let each actor i have an objective:

[
J_i(x_t,a_i).
]

Let institutions generate constraints and incentives:

[
u_t=G(x_t,\theta_t).
]

Then:

[
x_{t+1}

F(x_t,a_1,\ldots,a_n,u_t,\epsilon_t).
]

Now consider the feedback structure:

[
\boxed{
\text{measurement}
\rightarrow
\text{target}
\rightarrow
\text{optimization}
\rightarrow
\text{behavior}
\rightarrow
\text{new measurement}
}
]

This structure explains the modern escalation of metrics better than the simple metaphor of a game.

The central danger is not that someone cheats.

The danger is that:

[
\text{objective function}
]

becomes recursively embedded in:

[
\text{system dynamics}.
]

Once that happens, optimizing the objective changes the environment within which future optimization occurs.

---

XXII. The Real Mathematical Problem: Misaligned Objectives

The central mathematical structure can now be expressed compactly.

Let actual societal state be:

[
x\in X.
]

Let the genuine multidimensional objective be:

[
W(x).
]

Let institution i use a measurable proxy:

[
m_i(x).
]

The institution chooses:

[
a_i^\ast

\arg\max_{a_i}m_i(F(x,a_i)).
]

But society cares about:

[
a^\dagger

\arg\max_{a}W(F(x,a)).
]

There is no general theorem implying:

[
a_i^\ast=a^\dagger.
]

Indeed, if the metric produces externalities, irreversibility, delayed effects, or strategic adaptation, then divergence should be expected.

The fundamental failure is therefore:

[
\boxed{
\text{proxy optimization}
\neq
\text{reality optimization}.
}
]

The failure becomes systemic when many actors optimize different proxies simultaneously:

[
m_1,m_2,\ldots,m_n.
]

The system then becomes a superposition of locally rational controllers with potentially incompatible objective functions.

---

XXIII. Why “Optimize Yourself” Is Not a Solution

The standard response to structural problems is remarkably consistent:

«Improve your skills.»

«Become more productive.»

«Network.»

«Invest.»

«Learn to code.»

«Optimize your time.»

«Build your personal brand.»

«Increase your resilience.»

«Become more competitive.»

None of these actions is necessarily irrational.

The problem lies in treating them as universal explanations.

Suppose the structure is:

[
x_{t+1}=F(x_t,a_t).
]

Then optimizing a_t is useful only within the feasible set:

[
a_t\in A(x_t).
]

But structural conditions determine:

[
A(x_t).
]

A person may optimize perfectly within a bad feasible set.

Formally:

[
a_t^\ast

\arg\max_{a\in A(x_t)}J(a)
]

does not imply:

[
A(x_t)
]

itself is socially desirable.

This is the hidden political content of optimization rhetoric.

It asks the individual to optimize inside a state space whose boundaries they do not control.

---

XXIV. De-Gamification

The alternative is not to construct a larger scoreboard.

It is not to replace capitalism with a government KPI dashboard.

It is not to invent a “well-being score” that becomes the next object of optimization.

It is not to produce a more sophisticated ranking system.

That would reproduce the problem at a higher level.

De-gamification requires a change in the architecture of evaluation.

1. From proxy maximization to constraint preservation

Instead of:

[
\max GDP
]

or:

[
\max quarterly\ profit,
]

some dimensions should operate as constraints:

[
K_{\text{ecological}}\ge K_{\min},
]

[
H_{\text{human}}\ge H_{\min},
]

[
A_{\text{autonomy}}\ge A_{\min}.
]

The distinction matters.

A constraint is not another score.

A forest that must remain standing is not simply assigned ten points for “biodiversity.”

A human right should not become a KPI.

Some variables should define the feasible region itself.

---

2. From flow obsession to stock awareness

Economic accounting should distinguish:

[
\text{income}
]

from:

[
\text{wealth}.
]

Production flows should be evaluated against the condition of the stocks generating future production.

The World Bank's comprehensive wealth framework already points in this direction by measuring produced, human, natural, and financial assets alongside GDP.

The question changes from:

«Did we produce more this year?»

to:

«Did we leave the next generation at least as much productive and ecological capacity as we inherited?»

That is a radically different objective function.

---

3. From individual competition to collective-force accounting

Proudhon's insight should be retained in a modernized form.

Human achievement is produced through networks:

[
\text{individual capability}
+
\text{social infrastructure}
+
\text{knowledge inheritance}
+
\text{cooperation}
+
\text{public institutions}
+
\text{collective labor}.
]

The isolated “winner” is therefore an abstraction.

An engineer inherits mathematics.

A scientist inherits laboratories, instruments, language, libraries, teachers, standards, and centuries of accumulated theory.

A corporation inherits infrastructure, law, education systems, financial institutions, roads, telecommunications, and trained labor.

Individual productivity is therefore partly a crystallization of collective history.

That does not abolish individual contribution.

It makes individual contribution intelligible.

---

4. From symmetrical-player rhetoric to explicit power analysis

Every supposedly voluntary transaction should be capable of being analyzed in terms of:

[
P_i,
\quad
E_i,
\quad
I_i,
\quad
R_i,
]

where:

- P_i = bargaining power,
- E_i = exit capacity,
- I_i = information,
- R_i = ability to absorb risk.

Then the language of “choice” becomes empirically meaningful rather than ceremonial.

Two contracts may both be legally voluntary while possessing radically different power structures.

That is not semantic nitpicking.

It is the difference between contract as formal exchange and contract as lived constraint.

---

XXV. Diogenes Against the Final Scoreboard

The final Cynic question is therefore not:

«Who won?»

It is:

«Why did you believe the scoreboard?»

The modern system constantly produces objects of desire whose value depends upon collective recognition:

[
\text{rank}
\rightarrow
\text{prestige}
\rightarrow
\text{competition}
\rightarrow
\text{investment of attention}
\rightarrow
\text{reinforcement of rank}.
]

The loop is self-validating.

People compete because the ranking exists.

The ranking acquires importance because people compete.

The competition generates economic activity.

The activity is then cited as evidence that the ranking measures something important.

Diogenes interrupts the loop with the most primitive possible operation:

[
\boxed{\text{refusal to recognize the token}}
]

The Cynic does not necessarily destroy the currency.

He destroys its unquestioned authority.

That distinction is politically and philosophically decisive.

---

XXVI. Final Synthesis

The strongest version of the argument is therefore not:

[
\boxed{
\text{modern society is mathematically impossible}
}
]

That statement requires a complete formal model and proof.

Nor is it:

[
\boxed{
\text{markets are games and games are bad}
}
]

That collapses several different concepts into one.

The stronger argument is:

[
\boxed{
\text{Human existence cannot coherently be reduced to a competitive optimization game without losing essential properties of the system being described.}
}
]

The reduction fails along several independent dimensions.

Ontological failure

A person is not a score.

[
\text{person}\neq\text{metric}.
]

Epistemological failure

A proxy is not the reality it measures.

[
M\neq Q.
]

Dynamical failure

Optimizing a metric can change the system that the metric was supposed to measure.

[
\max M
\rightarrow
M\text{-driven behavioral change}.
]

Ecological failure

Flows can increase while underlying stocks deteriorate.

[
\Delta GDP>0
\not\Rightarrow
\Delta W_{\text{real}}>0.
]

The World Bank's comprehensive-wealth framework explicitly makes this stock-flow distinction central to sustainability analysis.

Distributional failure

Different initial states generate different feasible strategy spaces.

[
A=A(x_0).
]

A universal “player strategy” cannot erase unequal starting conditions.

Coordination failure

Individual optimization does not necessarily maximize system performance.

[
\arg\max_i J_i
\not\Rightarrow
\arg\max W.
]

Braess's paradox provides a precise model of this phenomenon.

Aggregation failure

There is no universally neutral procedure for converting arbitrary individual preferences into one social ordering while satisfying all of Arrow's conditions.

Temporal failure

Ensemble averages can diverge from individual time averages under non-ergodic dynamics.

[
\langle g\rangle_{\text{ensemble}}
\neq
g_{\text{time}}.
]

Peters' work makes this distinction central to the analysis of multiplicative wealth processes.

Power failure

A transaction between two formally consenting parties does not imply equality of bargaining power.

[
\text{consent}\neq\text{symmetry}.
]

Historical failure

Individual capability is built upon inherited collective infrastructure.

[
\text{individual output}
\neq
\text{individual causation alone}.
]

---

XXVII. The Central Proposition

The entire critique can ultimately be compressed into one proposition.

Let:

[
X_t
]

be the real multidimensional state of society.

Let:

[
M_t=f(X_t)
]

be a scalarized institutional metric.

Let agents optimize:

[
a_i^\ast=\arg\max M_i.
]

Suppose:

1. f is not a sufficient statistic for the dimensions of X that matter;
2. agents can modify X in ways that change the relation between M and the underlying state;
3. agents impose externalities on one another;
4. initial conditions are unequal;
5. some state variables are path-dependent or irreversible;
6. the institutional objective functions are not identical;
7. some participants control the rules or substrates faced by others.

Then there is no general reason to expect:

[
{a_i^\ast}_{i=1}^{n}
]

to produce:

[
\arg\max_X W(X).
]

Indeed, the system can rationally generate outcomes that nobody individually intends.

This is not irrationality.

It is distributed rationality operating on incompatible objectives inside a constrained dynamical system.

That is the real mathematical content behind the metaphor of a broken scoreboard.

---

Conclusion: Life Is Not a Game

The deepest error of gamified society is not that it encourages people to compete.

Competition can be playful, beautiful, intellectually productive, and voluntarily meaningful.

The error is treating existence itself as though it were voluntarily entered competition under neutral rules between interchangeable players.

It is not.

A child does not choose the starting conditions.

A human being cannot quit metabolism.

An ecosystem cannot negotiate its regeneration rate.

A depleted aquifer cannot accept a new strategy.

A lost year cannot be reloaded.

A social class does not begin from identical initial states.

An inherited fortune is not generated anew by the current player's cleverness.

A corporation does not merely “play” the market; it can possess infrastructure, capital, legal rights, information, and influence over the environment in which the game occurs.

A metric does not remain innocent after millions of people are forced to optimize it.

And a society is not made coherent merely because every participant has been told to maximize something.

The appropriate intellectual response is therefore neither nihilism nor another scoreboard.

It is de-gamification:

[
\boxed{
\text{from scores to states}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{from proxies to realities}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{from flows to stocks}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{from isolated players to collective production}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{from formal consent to analysis of actual power}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{from local optimization to system dynamics}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{from infinite growth narratives to finite physical constraints}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{from the scoreboard back to the world}
}
]

The Diogenean gesture is therefore still useful.

Deface the currency.

Not because every coin is worthless.

Because no coin should be allowed to decide what a human being is worth.
